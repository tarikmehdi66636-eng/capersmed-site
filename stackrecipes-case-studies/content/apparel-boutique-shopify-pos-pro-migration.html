The Baseline: The “SaaS App-Stacking” Trap

For five years this boutique ran a standard modern retail setup: a Shopify online storefront paired with Shopify POS Pro on two in-store counter iPads.

The checkout interface was smooth. The total cost of ownership had ballooned anyway, through incremental software rent that arrived one reasonable decision at a time:

Recurring software only. Card processing fees are separate and are not in these figures.
Subscription Monthly Annual
Shopify base plan $105 $1,260
Shopify POS Pro add-on (per location) $89 $1,068
Third-party inventory & matrix apps $65 $780
Total recurring software spend $259 $3,108

The third line is the one worth staring at. Those apps existed to do two things the platform would not: print custom barcode labels, and move stock between locations. Neither is exotic. Both were rented.

Note also what the middle line is not: POS Pro is billed per location, not per register. The second counter iPad is free; a second shop would not be. Any store modelling a move should check which of those two it is about to grow.

The friction that actually hurt

Beyond the monthly bill, the chief operational problem was inventory race conditions. On busy Saturdays a dress sold at the physical register took anywhere from 45 seconds to 4 minutes to sync by webhook to the online store. During seasonal drops that delay regularly caused double-selling: an online customer bought the same Medium linen dress that an in-store shopper was carrying out of the door in a bag.

The cost of that is not the refund. It is the email telling a customer the thing they just bought does not exist, on the day of a drop, about the one item they wanted.

The Architectural Shift: Single Database vs. API Bridges

Most retail stacks attempt omnichannel by bolting an independent eCommerce platform to an independent cash register through API connectors — Zapier, Make, or proprietary sync plugins. When network spikes or API rate limits occur, sync queues stall.

And a stalled queue is not an outage. That is what makes it dangerous. Both systems stay up, both keep taking money, and both keep answering the question “is this in stock?” from their own copy of the truth. The register says one Medium linen dress. The storefront says one Medium linen dress. There is one dress.

Every bridged architecture has the same three properties, and no amount of connector quality removes them:

  • The write is eventual. A sale is committed in one system and announced to the other. Between commit and announcement there is a window in which both will happily sell the same unit — and that window is a queue depth, not a constant, so it stretches exactly when you are busiest.
  • Retries are lossy at the edges. Rate limits, timeouts and 5xx responses put the event back on the queue. Well-built connectors retry with backoff and idempotency keys; the retry still lands after the window that mattered.
  • Reconciliation is manual. When the two copies disagree, something has to decide which is right. In practice that something is a person with a spreadsheet on Monday morning.

Deleting the second copy

The fix is not a better connector. By deploying Odoo 18 Community on a private VPS, the physical registers and the web storefront query the exact same PostgreSQL database.

When a cashier scans a barcode at the register:

  1. The transaction commits locally.
  2. The specific variant's quantity on hand (stock.quant) decrements instantaneously.
  3. If that was the last unit in stock, the web product page immediately updates to Out of Stock.

Zero synchronization queue, zero API polling limits. Two concurrent checkouts for the last Medium linen dress become two transactions racing for one row, and the database serialises them: one commits, the other is refused at the moment of sale — at the till, in front of the customer, where a substitution is an easy conversation.

The honest cost of this choiceOne database is one failure domain. Under bridged architecture, the storefront going down leaves the till trading; on a single Postgres instance, whatever takes the database down takes both. That trade is worth making for a single-location boutique, and it is worth making deliberately: it buys backups you have actually restored from, a tested offline mode at the till, and an answer to “what do we do if it is down at 4pm on a Saturday” that exists before the Saturday.

Catalog Migration: Untangling 3,400 Variant SKUs

Apparel catalogs are notorious for messy data exports. The boutique's Shopify export contained 3,400 active SKUs across dresses, denim, knitwear and jewelry, with up to three attribute dimensions (Size, Color, Fabric).

3,400 SKUs is not 3,400 products. It is a few hundred styles multiplied out across a size run, a colourway and sometimes a fabric. The multiplication is the whole problem: stock, barcodes and counts live on Medium / Sage / Linen, while the photograph, the description and the online product page live on Linen Shift Dress.

The normalization process

  1. Parent template grouping. We extracted the flat Shopify CSV and mapped items into parent product templates (product.template) linked to child variants (product.product). A Shopify export is variant-per-row with a shared handle, and the style-level fields are populated only on the first row of each group — read that file as a flat list and you get a few hundred correct products and three thousand blanks.
  2. Barcode deduplication. Over the years, staff had entered identical vendor UPC codes across different color variants. We isolated 42 duplicate barcodes before import, assigning unique internal EAN-13 barcodes to resolve scanner conflicts. A scan that cannot distinguish Medium from Large makes the count decorative, and no amount of discipline at the counter fixes it.
  3. Hangtag & jewelry label printing. Instead of paying for a monthly cloud label printing subscription, we configured direct printing to their desktop Zebra ZD420 label printer using standard ZPL commands over USB. That single change retires the larger half of the $65/month app line.

Two smaller items belong in the same pass, because they are free to fix during a migration and expensive afterwards: collapse the colour vocabulary against one controlled list (five years of buying produces Sage, sage green and Sage-Green as three distinct options), and set size sort order explicitly. XS, S, M, L, XL sorts correctly nowhere by accident, and a wrong sort order is visible in every size dropdown in the store.

Hardware Audit: What We Kept vs. What We Replaced

The store owner feared an entire hardware overhaul would wipe out any software savings. The audit kept 75% of the counter footprint:

Existing device Status Operational action
Apple iPad 10.2″ (×2) Kept Reused. Runs Odoo POS as a full-screen Progressive Web App in Safari.
Star TSP143III receipt printers Kept Assigned static IP addresses on the in-store router. Connected via port 9100 raw socket for silent printing.
Zebra DS2208 handheld scanners Kept Plug-and-play USB HID keyboard emulation. Zero drivers required.
Cash drawers (APG) Kept Plugged directly into the Star printer RJ12 kick port.
Shopify Tap & Chip readers Replaced Firmware-locked to Shopify. These cannot be repurposed on any other platform.

To replace the locked Shopify card readers, we deployed two BBPOS WisePOS E countertop smart terminals. These connect directly to the store's private Stripe account via Stripe Terminal. Card payments — chip, tap, Apple Pay, Google Pay — authorize on-screen, and funds settle directly into the boutique's commercial checking account without a Shopify intermediary gateway surcharge.

The firmware lock is the line to read twice if you are pricing your own move. Printers, scanners and drawers are generic devices that outlive the software driving them; a rented, platform-locked card reader is the one piece of counter hardware that is guaranteed to be worthless the day you leave.

The Cutover Execution

Retailers cannot afford downtime on a trading day. The cutover ran between Sunday close and Tuesday morning — a 40-hour window inside a two-day shutdown, with the whole of Monday held in reserve.

  1. Sunday 6:00 PM — Data freeze & final export Doors closed. We pulled the final inventory balance and customer records from Shopify. Shopify stays live and subscribed as the rollback.
  2. Sunday 8:30 PM — Database staging Cleaned CSV data imported into the private VPS instance. Variant matrices, tax categories (Texas state 6.25% + Austin city 2.0% = 8.25% total sales tax) and staff cashier PINs configured.
  3. Monday 10:00 AM — Hardware calibration & dry run Remote screen-share with the store manager. We bound the Stripe WisePOS E terminals, assigned local router IPs to the Star printers, and tested barcode scans against physical hangtags.
  4. Monday 2:00 PM — Staff walkthrough (30 minutes) Cashiers ran five simulated transactions: split tender (cash + card), customer returns, and applying line-item promotional discounts.
  5. Tuesday 10:00 AM — Live, doors open Store opened on schedule. Zero interrupted sales.

The gate this migration neededThe dry run is the place to prove the thing the whole project is for. Take a variant with exactly one unit in stock, open a web checkout and a till sale for it at the same time, and complete both. One must be refused. If a single unit sells twice on the bench, it will sell twice on a Saturday.

The Verdict

By trading software rent for private server ownership, this boutique recovered over $2,500 every single year into its operating margin, permanently solved web/in-store overselling, and gained full ownership of its 3,400-product database.

Where this blueprint does not apply

  • If you are about to open a second location. Multi-location changes the shape of the problem — transfers, per-site stock, staff permissions — and migrating a month before opening is the worst available timing.
  • If nobody owns the system. One database is one failure domain, and a private VPS is a server somebody has to patch. If that person does not exist and is not hired, the subscription is buying something real.
  • If the catalogue has never been reconciled. Forty-two duplicate barcodes were findable in an evening. A catalogue nobody has audited in five years can hide a fortnight of work, and that discovery belongs in the rehearsal, not in the window.
  • If the storefront is doing serious volume. A single-database cutover pauses trading on the web as well as in store. Above a certain daily order count the cost of the window exceeds three years of subscription, and the honest answer is to stay.
